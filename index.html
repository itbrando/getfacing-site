We get your products facing AI.

In a store, a facing is a product at the front of the shelf, turned to face the shopper; online, the shopper is increasingly an AI agent working for a person.

Facing AI finds where shopping agents get your products wrong or leave them out, why it happens, and what to change in the pages, catalogs and links you control so agents have the right product, size, price, stock and seller to show.

The shift to personal shopping agents

Agents have the reach. Sales through them are small today; forecasts put them at 15–25% of US online sales by 2030.

A personal shopping agent works for one person. It takes a request in that person’s words, such as a carry-on under $200 that fits a 22-inch limit and arrives by Friday, searches catalogs and stores, compares, and returns a short list with links; on some surfaces it also checks out. The person sees only the agent’s short list.

Shoppers still check its work. In Gartner surveys released in May 2026, consumers wanted AI help with shopping but not AI purchase decisions, and 54% of AI users said they double-check all the information.

How agents shop

Agents build answers from catalogs, search indexes and page reads.

Catalogs are the product data a store or platform sends an agent, usually as a merchant feed: a file listing each product with its price, stock and link. Search indexes come from the agent’s own crawler. Page reads happen live, as plain text or in a full browser.

In between, the agent turns one request into several searches, ranks candidates, and attaches a variant (a specific size, color or pack) and a seller to each. No agent documents how it ranks candidates or which variant and seller it attaches; a brand controls only the inputs. Some placements are paid: ChatGPT’s shopping results can include sponsored offers, and Google and Microsoft sell ads in their AI surfaces.

Where they fail

Agents attach another seller or the wrong product, and misread store pages.

Two more failures concern the store itself: its own data can disagree on price and stock, and its analytics can credit sales from agents to other channels. More on the mechanics: why agents quote the wrong price or stock.

  • Another seller holds the Visit button

    ChatGPT’s product panel shows one default offer, with a Visit button, above a list of other sellers. The default tracks a merchant-feed record: in 106 of 112 panels exactly one offer carried a feed tag (a marker in its link showing the offer came from a merchant feed), and it was always the default. A brand store without one drops into the list, even at the same price, and the click and the margin go to the seller holding the default; large retailers held 60 of the 79 defaults in panels with more than one seller.

    9 of 112

    panels made the product’s own brand store the default; in 77 the brand store was absent. Brand-store offers with a feed tag won 9 of 9, those without 0 of 26. Of the 19 that lost on the same product, 15 matched the winner’s price.

    ChatGPT · 112 product panels from 40 shopping questions · 2 Oct 2026

  • The link opens a different product or variant

    Agents group many sellers’ offers under one product. When the match is loose, a sibling model, another size or another pack lands on the card or among its other sellers; most often it is a sibling from the same product line. In ChatGPT, the “other sellers” list appears to come from a separate offer index, not the feed record behind the default, and it is where most of the wrong links we found sit. Shopify’s shared catalog, which feeds several agents, documents one way loose matching happens: it groups offers under one title and drops options “when an exact match isn’t available.”

    13.8%+

    of ChatGPT’s “other sellers” rows linked a different product or variant (30 of 217; 95% interval about 8–23%). Across all offers shown, at least 6.6% were wrong in a way that changes what the shopper gets (42 of 636; about 4–10%), nearly all a different product or variant.

    ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, Muse and OpenAI’s browser agent · 108 answers to general shopping questions · 27 Sep–6 Oct 2026 · 211 of the 217 “other sellers” rows from 2 Oct

  • Hidden page text reads as the product’s state

    Many store themes put a “Sold out” label or a “Notify me” button on every product page and hide it with styling until a variant sells out. Shoppers never see it. A page reader that runs no scripts and ignores styling still gets the text, and can report an in-stock product as sold out. Browser agents, which render the page, read the state as displayed.

    Example Asked about a product that is in stock, the agent calls it unavailable and puts another product first.

    30.7%

    of stores exposed a “Sold out”, “Out of stock” or “Notify me” label to a text-only reader on pages where every variant could be ordered (225 of 732), most of it a hidden badge in widely used theme code. 7.7% did so through a hidden “Notify me” button or back-in-stock form (56 of 732). This counts exposure, not how often agents act on it.

    Product pages of 732 Shopify stores · Sep 2026

  • The store’s own data disagrees on price and stock

    Structured data is the machine-readable product block in a page’s code; search engines read it for price and stock, and agents draw on search indexes. On most affected pages (84 of 101), every variant is marked in stock while some are sold out, a pattern that fits a template copying one stock state to every variant. Answers get prices wrong too: Gemini showed a compare-at (“was”) price 67% above the selling price, and put a product priced at $249 at “~$199”, inside a budget of under $200. Where Gemini got either price is not established.

    6.3%

    of stores’ structured data contradicted their own catalog on stock (41 of 654), nearly always by marking sold-out variants in stock; 1.4% on price, by more than 5% (9 of 654). These are store-side rates, not rates of wrong prices in answers.

    Product pages of 654 Shopify stores · Sep 2026

  • The sale is credited to the wrong channel

    Of the assistants we observed, only ChatGPT tags the links in its answers as its own (utm_source=chatgpt.com); OpenAI’s browser agent did not. Gemini’s links to brand stores often carry Google’s own click tag, so the visit looks like a click from Google’s free shopping listings, and a Muse card link we saw carried a Meta catalog tag. Google counts AI Mode clicks as organic search. Orders placed inside Copilot, Meta and Google show in Shopify as orders from that sales channel, but fire none of the store’s tracking pixels (the snippets that report visits and sales to its analytics and ad accounts). Much AI-driven traffic can end up credited to Google, Facebook, direct or unassigned.

    0 of 1,288

    links in Perplexity’s answers carried a tag naming it; Grok, 0 of 1,105; the Gemini app, 0 of 121; OpenAI’s browser agent, 0 of 6. ChatGPT tagged all 330 links in its product panels.

    Links in assistant answers · Sep–Oct 2026

About our numbers. Agent figures: answers from seven assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta AI, Muse, OpenAI’s browser agent, Perplexity and Grok) to shopping questions, mostly about outdoor gear and beauty, 27 September to 6 October 2026. Store figures: public product pages of 654 to 732 Shopify stores, September 2026. Error rates are minimums, since only confirmed errors count, so true rates may be higher. The questions are not a sample of real shopping traffic, other categories may differ, and no figure here measures an effect on sales.
