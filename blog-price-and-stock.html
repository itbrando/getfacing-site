Why AI shopping agents quote the wrong price or stock

A wrong price or a false “sold out” costs a sale at the moment a shopper decides. Five mechanisms account for most of it, and most start in data a brand controls.

Facing AIOctober 7, 20265-minute read

When an AI agent tells a shopper that a product costs less than it does, the shopper meets a higher price at checkout, or leaves. When it says an in-stock product is sold out, the shopper picks something else. Neither error shows up in a store’s reports, because the answer happens somewhere the store cannot see. These are the mechanisms we see most often, in agents’ answers and in stores’ own pages.

1. One name, several versions

Most products come in versions: sizes, colors, materials, packs. When versions are priced differently and share one product name, an agent has to settle on one. Platforms also merge listings for the same product. Shopify’s documentation for its global catalog, which several agents draw on, says results are “clustered by Universal Product ID”, and that when “an exact match isn’t available”, options in a shopper’s preferences are droppedSource 1. So a request for one version can come back attached to a sibling, with the sibling’s photo, price or link.

Illustration. A jacket comes in six colors, and two of them cost $40 more than the rest. Asked for the $189 navy jacket, an agent quotes $149, and its link opens the black one.

The ground for this error is common. On 54.6% of the Shopify stores we examined (458 of 839), at least one product’s price depends on a size or quantity option, and in 75% of those products the size is not in the titleSource 2. In ChatGPT’s “other sellers” rows, 13.8% linked a different product or variant (30 of 217)Source 2.

What a brand controls: one feed item per version, each with a title that names the version, its own photo, price and product code (GTIN), and a link that opens that version.

2. The first price a reader sees

A product page usually opens on a default version, and many show that version’s price, or a “from” price, until the shopper chooses. Agents read pages in different ways. ChatGPT’s page reader ran no scripts in our controlled testsSource 2: it reads the page’s code as delivered, not the page a shopper sees after choosing. A reader like that can take the first price it finds as the price of the version the shopper asked about. Browser agents render the page and can select a version, but only when the page makes that possible.

What a brand controls: each version’s price stated as text on the page, and links that open the version they name, with its price in the page’s code.

3. Data that disagrees with the store

Structured data is the machine-readable product description in a page’s code. Search engines read it for price and stock, and agents draw on search indexes. On 6.3% of the Shopify stores we examined (41 of 654), structured data contradicted the store’s own catalog on availability, nearly always by marking sold-out variants in stock; on 1.4% (9 of 654) it carried a price more than 5% offSource 2. A related error puts the compare-at (“was”) price where the selling price belongs: in one answer we recorded, Gemini showed a $119.99 compare-at price for an item selling at $71.99Source 2. Whether agents take price or stock from structured data has not been shown; the exposure is there either way.

What a brand controls: price and availability for each version in its structured data and feeds, generated from the same data as the page, with the compare-at price only in its own field.

4. Hidden “Sold out” text

Many store themes keep a “Sold out” label or a “Notify me” button in every product page’s code and hide it with styling until a version sells out. Shoppers never see it. A page reader that ignores styling still gets the words and can report an in-stock product as sold out. On product pages where every variant could be ordered, 30.7% of the Shopify stores we examined (225 of 732) still carried “Sold out”, “Out of stock” or “Notify me” text in the page’s code, mostly a hidden badge in common theme code; 7.7% (56 of 732) had a hidden sold-out button or back-in-stock formSource 2. Those figures measure exposure, not how often agents act on it. Browser agents read the state as displayed.

Illustration. A shopper asks whether a lamp comes in brass. Every finish is in stock, but the page’s code carries a hidden “Sold out” label, so the agent calls brass unavailable and recommends another brand’s lamp.

What a brand controls: theme elements that appear in the page’s code only when they are true, instead of being hidden with styling.

5. Another seller’s listing

When other stores list the same product, whether retailers, resellers, marketplaces or copycats, the agent may show their price, stock and link instead of the brand’s. In ChatGPT’s product panels, the default seller followed the merchant feed: a brand’s own store held the default every time its offer came through a feed (9 of 9) and never otherwise (0 of 26), and in 15 of the 19 cases where it lost on the same product, it matched the winning seller’s priceSource 2. Where several sellers were listed, large retailers held 60 of 79 defaultsSource 2. The price and stock the shopper sees are then the other seller’s.

What a brand controls: feed eligibility and channel settings on each platform, which partners may pass its products on, and product codes that let platforms match its own offer to the product.

Why it is hard to see from inside the store

None of these errors appears in a store’s reports. The answer happens elsewhere, and a shopper told the wrong price or stock may never visit. When a visit does come, most assistants do not label it: of those we observed, only ChatGPT tags its links as its ownSource 2. A brand learns about these failures only from what agents actually show, product by product and version by version, and what they show changes as agents and catalogs update.
