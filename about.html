A personal shopping agent works for one person. It takes a request in plain words, searches and compares on that person’s behalf, recommends a product from a particular seller and, on more and more surfaces, completes the purchase. The audience is already large: Google’s AI Mode has more than a billion monthly users and the Gemini app 950 millionSource 1, and about half of US adults now use AI chatbotsSource 2.

In 2026, agent connections became the default. Shopify made eligible merchants’ products discoverable in ChatGPT by default in MarchSource 7, and for eligible US Shopify stores, checkout inside Microsoft Copilot, Google’s AI Mode and Gemini, and Meta’s assistants is on by defaultSource 8. Amazon’s Buy for Me buys from other brands’ own sites for Amazon shoppersSource 9. ChatGPT went the other way on checkout: OpenAI scaled back Instant Checkout in March, and ChatGPT buyers now finish on the store’s own siteSource 15.

The traffic is small and growing fast. Independent studies put AI-referred visits at roughly 0.1–0.3% of traffic to online storesSources 12,13,14. Adobe measured AI-referred visits to US retail sites up 393% year over year in the first quarter of 2026 and 127% in AugustSource 10, and Shopify counted nearly 13 times as many AI-referred orders in the first quarter as a year earlierSource 11.

Forecasts are large. Bain projects that agents could start, influence or complete 15–25% of US online sales by 2030, $300–500 billionSource 3; Morgan Stanley’s range is 10–20%Source 4; McKinsey puts the ceiling at as much as $1 trillion of US retail revenueSource 5; and Juniper Research expects $1.5 trillion of agentic commerce spending worldwide in 2030Source 6. They count different things, which we compare on our blog. Shoppers still check the work: in Gartner’s surveys, 54% of AI users double-check “all information”Source 16.

02Where agents go wrong

Where agents get products wrong

An agent is only as good as what it can read and match. When it gets a product wrong, the shopper rarely knows, and the brand never sees it. The failures fall into a few kinds.

  • The wrong version

    The shopper asks for one size, color or pack, and the agent shows another, or a sibling model from the same line. Platforms merge many sellers’ listings under one product, and when a shopper’s choice has no exact match, options can be droppedSource 17.

  • The wrong price or stock

    A “was” price shown as the price, one version’s price quoted for another, an in-stock product called sold out, or a sold-out size offered as available. Some of it comes from page text a shopper never sees.

  • The wrong seller

    Another store’s listing holds the buy button, sometimes at the same price, or the brand’s own store does not appear at all. The click, the margin and the customer go elsewhere.

  • The missing fact

    A request turns on one detail, such as a weight limit, a shade or a compatible model. When that fact sits only in an image, or two fields disagree, the product drops out of contention.

  • The stalled checkout

    Agents that buy on a store’s own site can stop at a sign-in wall, a minimum order or a failed add to cart, and move on to another seller or brand.

  • The invisible sale

    Most assistants do not label the traffic they send, and orders placed inside an agent do not load a store’s analytics tags or ad pixelsSource 8. Sales that start with an agent are credited to search, social, direct or nothing.

03Why it’s hidden

Why brands can’t see it

Each agent draws on different sources: product catalogs that stores and platforms send it, its own search index, and pages it reads or browses live. None of them documents how it chooses the product, the version or the seller it shows. Answers change from day to day, and the error happens off the brand’s site, in a conversation the brand never sees. A store’s own reports cannot show what an agent told a shopper who never arrived.
