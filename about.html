How AI agents shop, and where they get products wrong

Facing AIOctober 202615-minute read

The shiftHow agents shopWhere they failWhat it costsWhat we doSources

Facing AI shows brands where AI shopping agents misrepresent or lose their products, why it happens and what to change. This memo is the analysis behind that work. Market figures in it are sourced and dated; our own figures state their scope.

  • In ChatGPT, the default “Visit” button on a product went to the seller whose offer came through a merchant feed, the file in which a store lists its products for a platform. Across 112 product cards on 2 October 2026, a brand’s own store held that button every time its offer came through a feed (9 of 9) and never otherwise (0 of 26), though in most of its losses it matched the winning seller’s price.
  • In 108 answers from five assistants to shopping requests that named no brand, at least 6.6% of offers were wrong (42 of 636); an offer is a product shown as buyable from a particular seller. Nearly all were the wrong product, or the right product in the wrong size or pack.

01The shift

Shopping is moving to personal AI agents, from a small base

A personal AI agent works for one shopper, though the companies that run these agents may also sell ads or, like Amazon, the products themselves. It takes a request in plain words, searches and compares on the shopper’s behalf, recommends a product from a particular seller and, on some surfaces, completes the purchase. The audience is large: about half of US adults use AI chatbotsSource 7, the Gemini app had 950 million monthly active users in July 2026Source 5, and Amazon says more than 300 million customers have used its Rufus assistantSource 8.

For Shopify stores, agent connections became the default in 2026. On 24 March, Shopify made eligible merchants’ products discoverable in ChatGPT by default, and it lists Microsoft Copilot, Google’s AI Mode and the Gemini app as live channels, with no fees beyond payment processingSources 9,10. Agents that operate a browser also buy on brands’ own sites: Amazon’s Buy for Me can “make purchases on behalf of customers” in other online storesSource 8.

Growth is fast, if slower than early in 2026. Adobe measured AI-referred visits to US retail sites up 393% year over year in the first quarter of 2026 and 127% in August, and it forecasts 130% growth for the holiday seasonSources 4,11. Shopify’s AI-referred orders in the first quarter were nearly 13 times the level of a year earlierSource 12, and its president said on the second-quarter call that AI traffic had “tripled”, with orders “right along with it”Source 13.

The base is small. Independent studies of online stores put AI-referred visits at roughly 0.1–0.3% of traffic: under 0.2% across 973 sites in a peer-reviewed study of August 2024 to July 2025, about a two-hundredth of the share from Google organic searchSource 1; 0.24% for 30 US brands in April 2026Source 2; and 0.10% of sessions (0.12% of revenue) across 10 Shopify stores from January to August 2026Source 3.

The conversion premium is recent and contested. Adobe found AI-referred visits converting 48% better than other traffic in August 2026, but 38% worse in March 2025; the peer-reviewed study found ChatGPT referrals converting below every traditional channel except paid socialSources 1,4. Neither figure shows a lift caused by AI. Where a premium appears, it most likely reflects who arrives: shoppers with intent, who land straight on a product page in more than half of AI sessionsSource 12.

Checkout is moving in two directions. In ChatGPT, checkout inside the chat has stepped back. On 24 March 2026, OpenAI scaled back Instant Checkout; Walmart had seen conversion in the chat three times lower than when shoppers clicked through to its siteSource 14. For Shopify merchants, purchases that start in ChatGPT now complete on the store’s own checkoutSource 9. Copilot, Meta and Google went the other way: for eligible US Shopify stores, checkout inside their assistants is on by defaultSources 10,16.

Forecasts are large but broadly defined. Bain projects $300–500 billion of US online sales by 2030, 15–25% of the total, counting purchases “initiated, influenced, or completed” by third-party or retailer-hosted agentsSource 6. Morgan Stanley’s range for “agentic shoppers” is 10–20% of US online spending, $190–385 billionSource 17. McKinsey’s estimate is the widest: “as much as $1 trillion” of US retail revenue orchestrated by agents, and $3–5 trillion worldwide, across all retail rather than online sales aloneSource 18. Juniper Research expects agentic commerce spending of $1.5 trillion worldwide in 2030, up from early trial deployments in 2025 and 2026Source 19. The spread comes from definitions: who counts as an agent, whether influence counts, and whether the base is online or all retail. We compare them in a note on our blog.

Today’s reported figures are smaller, and partly self-reported. Amazon credits Rufus with “nearly $12 billion in incremental annualized sales” in 2025, with no method givenSource 8. Shoppers remain cautious: in Gartner’s surveys, 54% of AI users double-check “all information”Source 15.

02How agents shop

How agents find a product, choose a seller and complete the sale

Agents draw on up to three kinds of outside input:

  • Catalogs: product databases that platforms build from merchant feeds. A feed lists each product with its variants (the sizes, colors or packs a shopper actually buys), prices and stock.
  • Search indexes: built by the platforms’ own crawlers.
  • Live page visits: by a page reader that takes the text from a page’s code, or by a browser the agent operates.

Platforms describe the step in between only in outline, if at all. Google tells merchants how often their products appear in its AI answers, and Bing shows site owners the searches behind Copilot’s citations; none documents which candidates are considered, how they are weighted or how a seller’s offer is attachedSource 16.

Two patterns stand out. Where a surface relies on merchant feeds, a brand outside the feed path (not eligible, or with the channel switched off) can be missing from that surface’s product cards, and a retailer’s feed can stand in for the brand’s own listing and take the buy button. In ChatGPT, the default followed the feed tag, and a matching price did not win it: a brand’s own store held the default every time its offer carried a feed tag (9 of 9) and never without one (0 of 26). In 15 of 19 cases where it lost the default for the same product, it matched the winner’s price, and once it was $50 cheaper and still lostSource 16.

Wrong products and variants account for about nine in ten confirmed errors; price errors are fewer partly because few prices could be checked. We group the failures at five points where a mechanism in the agent or platform meets an input the brand controls. The evidence differs by point, from errors in agents’ answers to controlled tests and counts across stores, and each point states its ownSource 16.

Scope of our figures. Offers: 636 offers in 108 answers from ChatGPT, Gemini, Meta’s Muse and Meta AI, and OpenAI’s browser agent, 27 September to 6 October 2026, to test requests that named no brand, mostly for outdoor gear and beauty products. The requests are not a sample of real shopping traffic; rates vary by category (8.8% for outdoor gear, 3.7% for beauty) and may differ in others. ChatGPT answers were unpersonalized, and 211 of its 217 “other sellers” rows are from 2 October. An offer counts as wrong only when the error was confirmed, so each rate is a lower bound. Links: answers from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Grok and the Gemini app, September and October 2026. Stores: public product pages of 654 to 839 Shopify stores, September 2026; these figures measure exposure to a defect, not harm, and the feed and page mechanisms behind them are not specific to Shopify. Tests: controlled pages, not live stores.

  • The offer is for a different product or size

    Platforms merge many sellers’ listings under one product. Shopify’s global catalog groups offers by a universal product ID under one title and drops options “when an exact match isn’t available”. ChatGPT’s “other sellers” appear to come from a separate index whose links look built for Google Shopping, and the most common error there attaches a sibling from the same line, a different model or size, to the product the agent recommended. How much of this a brand can prevent from its own data (distinct product codes, titles and variant fields) has not been testedSource 16.

    Observed cases. In ChatGPT, one of the “other sellers” listed for a 58-liter backpack linked the 48-liter version from the same line. In Gemini, the card for an answer’s top pick, a mascara, linked a different mascara from the same line.

    Measured in answers. Most confirmed errors (30 of 42) sat in ChatGPT’s “other sellers” rows. At least 8.7% of the product images in Gemini’s answers that link to a store were wrong (9 of 103, two of them price errors). Only 2 of 282 ChatGPT default cards were confirmed wrong, but for most the destination and price are unknown: a floor, not a low rate.

    Measured in stores. On 54.6% of the Shopify stores we examined (458 of 839), at least one product’s price depends on a size or quantity option; in 75% of those products, the size is not in the title.

    In the brand’s control: a distinct product code, title and link for each variant in its feeds, and which resellers list its products.

  • The price or stock is wrong

    An agent can show a compare-at (“was”) price as the price, or a price well off the store’s own. A store’s structured data, the machine-readable description of a product in a page’s code, can also contradict the store itself; whether agents take price or stock from it has not been shownSource 16.

    Observed cases (Gemini). A card showed a $119.99 compare-at price for an item selling at $71.99 before and after the answer. Asked for a jacket under $200, an answer showed one at about $199; the store had charged $249 for every size the day before.

    Measured in answers. Prices could be verified for only 28 offers, and 3 were wrong: cases, not a market rate.

    Measured in stores. On 6.3% of the Shopify stores we examined (41 of 654), structured data disagreed with the store’s own catalog on availability, nearly always by marking sold-out variants in stock; on 1.4% (9 of 654), it carried a materially wrong price.

    In the brand’s control: the price and stock that its structured data and feeds state for each variant. More in why agents quote the wrong price or stock.

  • Hidden page text can be read as the product’s state

    Many store themes keep a “Sold out” label or a “Notify me” button in every product page’s code and hide it until a size sells out. Shoppers never see it. A page reader that takes the text from a page’s code without rendering it, as a browser would, keeps that text and can report an in-stock product as sold out. ChatGPT’s page reader ran no scripts in controlled testsSource 16.

    Measured in stores. On product pages where every variant could be ordered, 30.7% of the Shopify stores we examined (225 of 732) still carried “Sold out”, “Out of stock” or “Notify me” text in the page’s code, mostly a hidden badge in common theme code; 7.7% (56 of 732) had a hidden sold-out button or back-in-stock form. These figures measure exposure, not how often agents act on it.

    In the brand’s control: theme elements that appear in the page’s code only when they are true.

  • The deciding fact is missing or contradicted

    A request with a constraint (a weight limit, a shade, a pack size, a size 12) turns on one attribute. When that fact appears only in an image or inside a widget, or when two fields disagree, the product can drop out of contentionSource 16.

    Measured in controlled tests. Removing a contending product’s specifications cut how often it was picked by 60.6 percentage points. A product whose page carried a second, conflicting weight was picked in 15 of 72 trials; with that field removed, in 68 of 72.

    Measured in stores. In 65% of Shopify products that state a weight in both the catalog and the description (5,686 of 8,747), the two differ by more than 20%, nine times in ten with the catalog heavier. A third of the disagreeing catalog weights are whole pounds or kilos, which look like shipping weights or placeholders. Whether feeds pass that field to agents is unknown.

    In the brand’s control: the deciding facts in visible text, feed fields and titles, with no second field that disagrees.

  • The sale goes to another seller, or stalls at checkout

    In ChatGPT, the default button goes to the offer that came through a merchant feed (section 02), so a brand outside that feed path appears, if at all, among the other sellers. Selling on a platform that passes products to AI channels by default does not settle it: in 5 cases, a brand’s own Shopify store lost the default for its own product to another store whose link carried a feed tag. In earlier tests, agents that operate a browser switched to another seller or brand at a minimum order, a sign-in wall or a failed add to cartSource 16.

    Measured in answers. Default rows never showed a shipping line (0 of 112), while brand-store rows that lost the default showed one in 24 of 26, which suggests the two kinds of row come from different sources. Where several sellers were listed, large retailers held 60 of 79 defaults.

    In the brand’s control: feed eligibility and channel settings on each platform, which partners may pass its products on, and a checkout an agent can complete.

04What it costs

What this costs a brand

None of these failures has a measured effect on sales, and we do not claim oneSource 16. The costs are of three kinds.

  • Lost or misdirected sales

    A shopper told that a product is sold out, or shown the wrong size or price, can buy something else or nothing. A shopper sent to a retailer’s listing is likely to buy there: a brand that sells to that retailer earns at best its wholesale price, and the retail margin and the customer go to the retailer. In the ChatGPT panels above, the product’s own brand store was not listed in 77 of 112; where it was listed, it held the default in about a quarter to a third of its appearancesSource 16.

  • Wrong orders

    When the agent completes the purchase itself, through a platform checkout or a browser on the brand’s site, a wrong variant on the card can become a wrong orderSource 16.

  • Revenue credited to the wrong channel

    A UTM tag is a label in a link’s address that tells analytics where a visit came from. Of the assistants we observed, only ChatGPT tags its links as its own (utm_source=chatgpt.com). Perplexity tagged none of 1,288 links, Grok none of 1,105 and the Gemini app none of 121, so analytics can recognize their visits only by the referring site, when the browser passes it on. Most of Gemini’s links to brand stores carry a click ID, a code Google adds to its links, so they look like visits from Google’s unpaid product listings. A Muse card link carried a Meta catalog tag but no source tag, and clicks from AI Mode count as organic search. Orders placed inside Copilot, Meta and Google appear in Shopify under their own sales channel, but they do not load the store’s analytics tags or ad pixels in the shopper’s browser. A brand that reads AI’s share from analytics alone will undercount itSource 16.

The visible money is small today: across 10 Shopify stores, AI-referred visits brought 0.12% of revenue from January to August 2026, and the study’s most generous allowance for untagged ChatGPT visits lifts that only to 0.19%Source 3. The stake lies elsewhere. At those stores, 85% of AI-referred revenue came from first-time customers, against 42% of their revenue overallSource 3; part of AI’s influence carries no AI label, as above; and the channel is still growing fast, if more slowly than early in 2026, while catalog connections and platform checkouts switch on by defaultSources 4,10,13.

05What we do

What Facing AI does for a brand

Facing AI shows a brand where AI shopping agents misrepresent its products or send shoppers to other sellers, why each case happens, and what to change in the sources the brand controls: its catalog and feeds, its product pages and structured data, and the listings its partners pass on to AI platforms. It also shows which errors only the agent’s maker can fix. Once a change is live, we confirm that it holds in what the agents show.

Product and size
Which agents show another product or size in place of the brand’s own, and where the mix-up starts.
Price and stock
Where agents show a price or stock state the store does not, and which of the brand’s sources disagrees.
Hidden page text
Which pages show page readers a state that shoppers never see.
Deciding facts
Which facts each product needs to stay in contention, and where they are missing or contradicted.
Seller and checkout
Who holds the buy button for the brand’s products, and where an agent’s purchase stops.

On a shelf, a facing is the product turned toward the shopper. In an agent’s answer, it is the product, size and seller the agent puts forward.
